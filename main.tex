% ---- Abstract text ----
\paragraph{Summary.}
Foraging requires integrating sensory cues, reward history, and environmental context to guide adaptive decisions.
We developed a head-fixed virtual reality patch foraging task in which mice traverse a linear track and make self-initiated decisions to harvest probabilistic water rewards at odor-cued sites (Fig.~1A--C).
Patches deplete exponentially with harvesting, and at each odor site mice decide whether to stop and exploit or run and leave the patch, yielding rich behavioral sequences including short visits, long visits, and skipped patches (Fig.~1D).

Naive mice rapidly acquired the task, learning to use odor identity and reward outcomes to guide patch-leaving decisions.
Mice consistently stopped at rewarded patch types and ran through the unrewarded patch (Fig.~1B), and velocity profiles at odor onset differed systematically by patch type (Fig.~1C).
Within rewarded patches, mice were more likely to leave after consecutive reward omissions in the low-value patch than the high-value patch (Fig.~1F), demonstrating odor-cued expectations about patch quality.
On average, mice left both rewarded patch types at equivalent reward probabilities, consistent with the Marginal Value Theorem (MVT), but inconsistent with simpler heuristics such as reward counting or win-stay/lose-shift strategies.

To capture how mice estimate and use a global reward rate, we formulated a Bayesian MVT model (Fig.~2C) in which animals maintain patch-type-specific beliefs initialized from odor-based priors and updated by within-patch outcomes.
The model reproduces the key behavioral signature: reward probability at leaving is matched across patch types (n.s.), while all other metrics differ significantly (Fig.~2D--E).
Trial-to-trial variability in patch-leaving arises naturally from posterior uncertainty.
Manipulations of inter-patch travel distance shifted patch-leaving thresholds in the directions predicted by MVT (Fig.~2F--G).
Critically, changing reward statistics in one patch type shifted leaving decisions in the other---a cross-patch generalization establishing that mice integrate reward information globally across the environment to guide local patch-leaving decisions.

This task and behavioral framework establish a foundation for identifying the circuit mechanisms underlying global reward rate estimation in naturalistic foraging.

\paragraph{Additional Detail.}
Continuous behavior decomposed into stereotyped kinematic motifs---identified using hidden Markov models fitted to velocity and respiration---organized across odor sites, patches, and sessions (Fig.~1A--G).
The stop action in rewarded patches was highly stereotyped across mice, with sharp deceleration upon odor onset followed by sustained stopping and reward collection; patch-leaving decisions showed a characteristic velocity signature that differed between early and late patch departures (Fig.~1E).
Patch-level choice sequences (Fig.~1A) reveal that mice integrated reward history within patches across the session.

The Bayesian MVT model (Fig.~2A--C) formalizes how an optimal forager should leave patches given uncertainty about patch quality.
The agent maintains separate posteriors over reward probability for each patch type, updated after each binary outcome with a forgetting parameter $\delta < 1$ enabling rapid within-patch updating.
At each odor site, a sample from the current posterior is compared to a running estimate of global reward rate $\bar{R}$; the agent leaves when the sampled local rate falls below $\bar{R}$.
This Thompson-sampling rule produces stochastic leaving behavior whose variability is inherited from posterior uncertainty.
The model captures both average behavior (Fig.~2D--E) and session-level shifts in leaving thresholds following reward rate manipulations.

Three independent manipulations confirm that mice track a global reward rate.
First, increasing inter-patch travel distance lowered the global reward rate, causing mice to leave patches at lower reward probabilities; shorter distances had the opposite effect (Fig.~2F--G).
Second, increasing wheel friction during inter-patch travel produced equivalent shifts without affecting within-patch kinematics.
Third, changing reward probabilities in one patch type shifted leaving thresholds for both patch types---including the unchanged control patch---demonstrating cross-patch generalization of the global rate estimate.
This signature distinguishes our Bayesian MVT account from purely local threshold rules.

Preliminary electrophysiology recordings reveal structured population dynamics during task performance.
PCA trajectories separate distinct task epochs (odor onset, stopping, reward delivery) and differ by patch type (Fig.~2H), suggesting that neural state reflects the animal's current foraging context.
Population-level PSTHs reveal heterogeneous functional cell types, with subpopulations responding preferentially to odor onset, reward outcome, or patch-leaving events (Fig.~2I).
These early results motivate ongoing multi-regional Neuropixels recordings to identify the circuit mechanisms underlying global reward rate estimation.
