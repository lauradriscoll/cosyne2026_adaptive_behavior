\noindent\textbf{Summary: }Natural decisions unfold through continuous interaction between an organism and its environment. Foraging is an innate yet flexible behavior where animals repeatedly decide whether to exploit a depleting patch or leave to explore alternatives. We developed a head-fixed virtual reality patch foraging task that preserves the hierarchical and self-paced structure of natural behavior while allowing experimental control and access. Mice traversed a virtual linear track and made self-initiated choices to harvest probabilistic water rewards at odor-cued sites. Odors cued three patch types: high- and low-value patches whose reward probabilities depleted with each collected reward, and an unrewarded patch. Naïve mice rapidly acquired the task, learning to use odor identity and reward outcomes to guide patch-leaving decisions in fewer than seven sessions on average. We used multi-timescale hidden markov models to identify hierarchically structured motifs in running velocity and respiration. Within a patch, run, approach, stop, and leave motifs occurred in sequence, and shifted as mice approached patch leaving, reflecting a latent decision variable. Individual mice had characteristic engagement state dynamics across sessions. Mice left both rewarded patch types at equivalent reward probabilities, consistent with optimal patch leaving decisions as predicted by the Marginal Value Theorem (MVT, Charnov 1976), but inconsistent with mice using simpler heuristics such as reward counting or win-stay/lose-shift strategies. Independent manipulations of travel cost or global reward rate shifted patch-leaving decisions as predicted by MVT, demonstrating that mice use global reward rate to set local patch leaving thresholds. A Bayesian MVT model (McNamara and Houston 1980) with two timescales of learning reproduced mouse behavior in steady-state and dynamic conditions. This task offers an avenue to investigate brain-wide circuit dynamics for adaptive behavior anchored in normative foraging theory.  \\
